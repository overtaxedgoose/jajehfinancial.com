Residential Construction

New home builders, general contractors, remodelers and home services companies: job costing that tells the truth, cash that survives the draw schedule, and a company worth more than its last project.

Book a Discovery Call

A construction company can be profitable on paper and broke at the bank in the same month. Revenue arrives on the lender’s or the homeowner’s schedule, costs arrive on the crew’s, and every job carries its own margin story that the company P&L averages away. The accounting has to work at the job level, in real time, or the first warning of a losing project is the final invoice.

The core disciplines are job costing against the estimate, revenue recognized as the work is done rather than as deposits are collected, and a cash forecast built around draws, retainage and deposits. Add field payroll, subcontractor compliance and the lender’s paperwork, and the books become the operating system of the business. Home services companies live by the same rules at higher volume: hundreds of small jobs, dispatch and technician productivity, maintenance agreements and inventory on trucks.

What the Books Have to Handle

Job Costing vs. Estimate

Every labor hour, material, subcontractor and equipment charge coded to a job and a cost code, compared to the estimate every week. Overruns get caught at thirty percent complete, while a change order is still possible, not at closeout.

Revenue Recognition & WIP

Percentage-of-completion for contracts, with over- and under-billings tracked on a monthly WIP schedule. Deposits are liabilities, not income. Builders who book cash as revenue see phantom profit in deposit-heavy months and losses when the work gets done.

Draws, Retainage & Deposits

Construction loan draw requests, retainage held and receivable, customer deposits tracked by contract, and lien waivers tied to every payment. The cash forecast runs on the draw schedule, not the invoice date.

Change Orders & Allowances

Scope changes priced, signed and billed before the work is done, and allowance overages tracked and recovered. Unbilled change orders are the most common place a builder leaves money on the table.

Payroll, Subs & Compliance

Field and office payroll, certified payroll where it applies, workers’ comp classified by trade, subcontractor insurance certificates and 1099s, and the sales tax rules on materials versus labor that vary by state.

Home Services Economics

For HVAC, plumbing, electrical, roofing and repair companies: technician productivity, revenue per truck, maintenance agreement accounting, truck inventory and marketing cost per booked call.

The Numbers That Drive Profitability

The numbers that separate a contractor with a good year from a construction company that compounds. Most builders know their volume; fewer know these.

Gross Margin by Job

Contract value minus direct costs, by job and by job type. Averaged across the company it hides the losers; by job it shows which work to chase.

Builders 20 to 25%, remodelers 30 to 40%, service work 50%+

Net Profit Margin

What is left after overhead. Many contractors run on 2 to 5% without knowing it until tax time.

8 to 10% or better

Estimate Accuracy

Actual job cost against the estimate, by cost code. Within 5% means your estimating is a profit center; past 10%, every bid is a coin flip.

Within 5% of estimate

Over and Under Billings

Billings to date against earned revenue on every open contract. Under-billings are work you are financing; over-billings are cash you have already spent.

WIP schedule monthly, every variance explained

Backlog and Cash Runway

Signed contracts not yet performed, in months of revenue, against the cash and credit available to carry them.

3 to 6 months of backlog, 2+ months of overhead in cash

Break-Even Revenue

Fixed overhead divided by average gross margin: the volume you need before the company earns a dollar. It moves with every hire and every truck.

Known every month, reset after every hire
