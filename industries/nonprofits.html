Nonprofits

Fund accounting, donor and grant compliance, and audit readiness that let the board lead with the mission instead of the spreadsheet.

Book a Discovery Call

Nonprofits run on a harder set of books than most for-profit companies their size. Every dollar carries a restriction, a funder, a reporting deadline and a public Form 990, and the audit comes every year whether the staff is ready for it or not.

Done well, nonprofit accounting is also a fundraising asset. A clean audit, a readable 990 and board reports that show program cost per outcome are what sophisticated funders look at before they write the larger check. We take on nonprofit clients selectively, because the organizations that benefit most are the ones ready to treat financial management as part of the mission.

What the Books Have to Handle

Fund Accounting & Restrictions

Net assets with and without donor restrictions, tracked by fund and by purpose, with releases from restriction recorded as the work is done. The board should know at a glance how much of the cash on hand is actually theirs to spend.

Functional Expense Allocation

Every expense split across program, management and general, and fundraising using a documented, defensible method: time studies, square footage, headcount. It feeds the 990, the audit and every watchdog rating.

Grant Management

Allowable costs, matching requirements, reporting calendars and the cash gap that reimbursement-basis grants create. Federal awards above the Single Audit threshold add Uniform Guidance compliance on top.

Donor Compliance

Written acknowledgments for gifts of $250 or more, quid pro quo disclosures, pledges receivable, in-kind valuation and donor-advised fund receipts, all documented the way the IRS and the auditors expect.

Audit & Form 990 Readiness

Monthly closes, reconciled accounts, segregation of duties that works with a small staff, and a 990 the board has reviewed before it becomes a public document. Funders read the 990; it should tell the story you want told.

Board & Multi-Level Reporting

The board, the finance committee, program directors and each funder need a different cut of the same numbers: budget to actual by program, cash and reserves, restricted balances, and dashboards a non-accountant can read in five minutes.

The Numbers That Drive Profitability

“No margin, no mission.” Nonprofits need surplus and reserves as much as any business; these are the gauges that tell a board whether the organization is sustainable.

Operating Surplus

Revenue minus expenses from ordinary operations, before gifts restricted for future years. The only way reserves get built.

3 to 5% or better in a normal year

Months of Operating Reserve

Unrestricted, liquid net assets divided by average monthly expenses. The cushion between a late grant payment and a missed payroll.

3 to 6 months

Program Expense Ratio

Program spending as a share of total expenses. Funders and watchdogs watch it, but starving infrastructure to inflate it is a false economy.

65 to 75% or better, with honest overhead

Revenue Concentration

The share of total revenue from the largest funder or source. One lost grant should not be an existential event.

No single source above 25 to 30%

Grant Receivable Days

Average days between spending reimbursable dollars and receiving them. The number that sizes your reserve or your line of credit.

Known for every grant, and funded

Cost per Program Outcome

Total program cost divided by units of service delivered. The number that turns a budget conversation into a mission conversation.

Tracked by program, trending down
