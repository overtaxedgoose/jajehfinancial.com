Creative Agencies

Ad agencies, design studios and production companies: know which projects make money, which clients cost you, and when you can afford the next hire.

Book a Discovery Call

Agencies sell time and ideas, so the top line can look healthy right up until a project runs forty percent over scope. The books have to answer a harder question than “how much did we bill?” They have to answer how much of that was really yours, and what it cost you to earn it.

The first discipline is separating pass-through revenue (media buys, freelancers, printing, production rentals) from agency gross income, the money that actually pays your people. The second is tying every hour on the timesheet to a job in the ledger, so project margin is a fact instead of a feeling. With those two in place, forecasting cash and planning hires stops being guesswork, and the agency can grow on purpose.

What the Books Have to Handle

Project Profitability

Job costing that ties timesheet hours and direct costs to each project, with estimated-versus-actual hours by phase, so scope creep shows up while you can still bill for it instead of in the retrospective.

Pass-Through vs. Net Revenue

Media, freelancers, printing and production costs flow through the agency at little or no margin. Booking them as revenue inflates the top line and hides the real margin. We report agency gross income separately so every ratio means something.

Revenue Recognition & WIP

Retainers, milestones and percentage-of-completion work each recognize revenue differently. Deferred revenue, unbilled work in progress and over-servicing all need a home in the ledger, or the P&L swings with the billing calendar instead of the work.

Cash Flow Timing

Clients pay in 45 to 90 days; payroll runs every two weeks and vendors want deposits up front. A rolling 13-week cash forecast, deposit and milestone-billing policies, and a properly sized line of credit keep growth from turning into a liquidity crisis.

Capacity & Hiring Forecasts

Utilization by role, pipeline-weighted revenue and the contractor-versus-employee math behind each hire. The goal is to add people a quarter before you need them, not a quarter after.

Payroll, Contractors & Compliance

Salaried creatives, hourly producers, 1099 freelancers, commissions and bonus pools, multi-state remote staff, and sales tax exposure on tangible and digital deliverables that varies by state.

The Numbers That Drive Profitability

Rules of thumb for a healthy agency. Your targets depend on the mix of retainer and project work, but these are the gauges we watch every month.

Agency Gross Income

Revenue minus pass-through costs: the money that is actually yours. Growth in AGI, not billings, is real growth, and it is the denominator for every ratio below.

Measured against AGI, not billings

Delivery Labor as % of AGI

Salaries, benefits and freelance costs for the people who produce the work. The single biggest lever on agency margin.

50 to 60% of AGI

Net Margin

What is left after delivery labor and overhead. Below 10% the agency is one lost client away from trouble; above 20% it is building wealth.

15 to 25% of AGI

Billable Utilization

Billable hours divided by available hours for delivery staff. Too low and you are overstaffed; too high and quality and retention slip.

65 to 75% for delivery roles

Effective Hourly Rate

AGI divided by all hours worked, billed or not. The real price you are getting for an hour, and the fastest way to see over-servicing.

Rising quarter over quarter

Client Concentration

The share of AGI from your largest client. Concentration is fine until that client reviews the account.

No client above 20 to 25% of AGI
