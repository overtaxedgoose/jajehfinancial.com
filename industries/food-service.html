Food Service

Restaurants, cafes, coffee shops, caterers and grocery stores: prime cost you can see every week, cash and tips handled right, and a path from one profitable location to the next.

Book a Discovery Call

Food service runs on thin margins and fast money. Sales land every day, food is bought and sold inside the week, payroll is the biggest check and the most complicated one, and a few points of waste or a loose schedule is the difference between a good year and a loss. A monthly P&L that arrives three weeks after the month ends is too late to fix any of it.

The disciplines are weekly: prime cost reported against sales while the week is still fresh, a four-week or thirteen-period calendar so periods compare to periods, inventory counted and costed, cash and tips reconciled to the point of sale, and sales tax and payroll compliance handled before they turn into penalties. With those in place the operator can see which menu items, dayparts and locations make money, and the next location, the catering arm or the wholesale account becomes a decision instead of a gamble. We keep these books today for grocery stores, cafes, coffee shops, full-service restaurants and caterers, and each one keeps score a little differently.

What the Books Have to Handle

Prime Cost, Every Week

Food, beverage and labor cost together against sales, by week and by location, on a four-week or thirteen-period calendar so comparisons hold up. Prime cost is the number that decides whether the operation works, and it has to be on the owner’s desk on Monday, not at month end.

POS, Cash & Tips

Daily sales from the point of sale reconciled to deposits, card settlements, delivery-platform payouts and cash on hand, with voids, comps and discounts reviewed. Tips pooled and paid under federal and state rules, the FICA tip credit captured, and every dollar traced from the ticket to the bank.

Inventory & Cost of Goods

Period-end counts valued at current cost, so cost of goods sold reflects what was used rather than what was purchased. Theoretical versus actual cost by category exposes waste, portioning and theft. For grocers it is thousands of SKUs, department margins, shrink and vendor rebates, at scale.

Payroll & Labor Compliance

Tipped minimum wage and tip credits, overtime across roles and locations, schedules written against the sales forecast, and the health coverage, workers’ comp and multi-state rules that follow a growing headcount. Labor is the largest controllable cost and the one most tangled in compliance.

Sales Tax, Deposits & Gift Cards

Prepared food taxed differently from grocery items, delivery-platform and marketplace rules, catering deposits held as liabilities until the event, service charges that are not tips, and gift cards carried as liabilities until they are redeemed.

Multi-Unit & Expansion Economics

A P&L for every location, shared costs allocated by a written policy, a new-unit model that covers build-out, pre-opening costs and the ramp, and leases with percentage rent and tenant improvement allowances accounted for properly. The second location should be opened on the first location’s numbers.

The Numbers That Drive Profitability

Rules of thumb for a healthy operation. Targets vary by concept, from a coffee bar to full-service dining to a grocery floor, but these are the gauges we watch every week.

Prime Cost

Cost of goods sold plus total labor, as a share of sales. The single number that says whether the operation can make money, and the first thing we look at every week.

Under 60 to 65% of sales, by concept

Food & Beverage Cost

Cost of goods sold as a share of sales, by category: food, coffee, beer, wine, liquor, retail. Compared to the theoretical cost from recipes and the menu mix.

Restaurants 28 to 35%; grocers manage to a 25 to 30% gross margin

Labor Cost

Total labor with payroll taxes and benefits, as a share of sales, scheduled against the forecast by daypart.

Restaurants 25 to 35%; grocers 12 to 15%

Sales per Labor Hour

Net sales divided by hours worked. The productivity measure that keeps the schedule honest when sales dip.

Known by daypart, rising faster than wages

Occupancy Cost

Rent, common-area charges, property taxes, insurance and utilities as a share of sales. Fixed on the day the lease is signed, which is why the lease is a financial decision first.

6 to 10% of sales

Net Profit Margin

What is left after prime cost, occupancy and overhead. Thin by nature, so volume, pricing and waste matter more here than in almost any other industry.

Restaurants 5 to 10%; grocers 1 to 3% on volume
